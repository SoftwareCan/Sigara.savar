Before you quit

It can be hard to work everything out on quit day. Preparing ahead can make decisions easier, especially at the times when you would usually smoke without thinking.

  1. Set your quit day

    Pick a clear date for yourself and write down the preparations you want to finish before it.

  2. Track your smoking pattern

    Observe for a few days at what time, with what emotion, with whom, and in what setting you smoke. This record helps you see your strongest triggers.

  3. Arrange your surroundings

    Remove objects that remind you of smoking — packs, ashtrays, lighters — from home, car, and work. Choose a new behavior for strong pairings like coffee, after meals, or work breaks.

  4. Ask for support

    Telling people close to you about your decision to quit, and asking them not to offer you cigarettes especially in the first days, can make the process easier.

For difficult moments

Choosing what you will do before a craving arrives leaves you with fewer decisions to make in the moment.

  1. Delay

    when the craving hits, instead of acting right away, delay the decision to smoke for a few minutes.

  2. Deep breathe

    focus on easing the bodily tension with slow, controlled breathing.

  3. Drink water

    a few sips of water can place a simple pause in between and interrupt the automatic smoking behavior.

  4. Distract

    change your setting, take a short walk, call someone, or shift your mind to something else.

What changes in your body?

General health benefits of quitting, from the first minutes to the longer term.

Selected from the app’s health goals and checked against WHO, NHS and CDC sources. These timings are a general guide; individual health circumstances vary.

See 10 health milestones
  1. 20 minutes

    Heart rate and blood pressure begin to return towards normal.

    The first changes may begin within minutes of the last cigarette.

    World Health Organization (WHO)
  2. 12 hours

    Carbon monoxide in the blood returns to the normal range.

    WHO’s general timeline places this change at around 12 hours.

    World Health Organization (WHO)
  3. 48 hours

    Your senses of taste and smell may begin to improve.

    The NHS highlights improvements in taste and smell within the first two days.

    NHS · Better Health
  4. 72 hours (3 days)

    The bronchial tubes relax; breathing may become easier.

    The NHS includes this among the general benefits at around 72 hours.

    NHS · Better Health
  5. 2 weeks

    Blood circulation may improve.

    WHO’s timeline places these benefits within a 2–12 week period.

    World Health Organization (WHO)
  6. 1 month

    Coughing and shortness of breath may decrease over time.

    WHO describes these changes over a period of 1–9 months.

    World Health Organization (WHO)
  7. 3 months

    Lung function may improve and respiratory symptoms may ease.

    The NHS describes these benefits over 3–9 months; change does not happen on one fixed date.

    NHS · Better Health
  8. 1 year

    The risk of coronary heart disease falls to about half.

    WHO’s comparison is with someone who continues to smoke.

    World Health Organization (WHO)
  9. 10 years

    The risk of dying from lung cancer falls to about half.

    The comparison in the NHS’s general timeline is with someone who continues to smoke.

    NHS · Better Health
  10. 15 years

    The risk of coronary heart disease approaches that of a non-smoker.

    The CDC includes this among the longer-term benefits seen after about 15 years.

    CDC · Benefits of quitting smoking

Sources: World Health Organization (WHO) · NHS · Better Health · CDC · Benefits of quitting smoking
